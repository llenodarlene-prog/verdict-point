# US Bankruptcy Statistics: Filings by Chapter and Case Type

US bankruptcy courts received 621,111 filings in the 12 months ending September 30, 2026, up 11.4% from the year before. Non-business cases made up 594,057 of them. Chapter 7 was the largest category, with 391,386 filings.

These bankruptcy statistics count court cases, not people or companies. A joint consumer petition can cover two spouses, while one business failure can produce several related cases. Keep that unit in mind when you read every figure below.

All numbers come from the Administrative Office of the US Courts. We matched each one to the source release and Table F on November 13, 2026.

## Key Takeaways

- **Filings Rose Again:** Courts received 621,111 bankruptcy filings in the year to September 2026, up 11.4%.
- **Mostly Consumers:** Non-business cases were 594,057, about 95.6% of the total.
- **Chapter 7 Leads:** Chapter 7 had 391,386 filings, about 63% of all cases.
- **Still Below 2010:** Filings remain far under the nearly 1.6 million recorded in the year to September 2010.
- **Pending Cases Grew:** Pending cases rose 9.0% to 749,734, because filings outpaced terminations.

## Five Years of Bankruptcy Filings

The judiciary's [October 2026 bankruptcy release](https://www.uscourts.gov/data-news/judiciary-news/2026/10/22/bankruptcy-filings-increase-114-percent) gives five years of totals. Each year ends September 30.

| Year Ending Sept. 30 | Business | Non-Business | Total |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2022 | 13,125 | 370,685 | 383,810 |
| 2023 | 17,051 | 416,607 | 433,658 |
| 2024 | 22,762 | 481,350 | 504,112 |
| 2025 | 24,039 | 533,337 | 557,376 |
| 2026 | 27,054 | 594,057 | 621,111 |

These are rolling 12-month periods, not calendar years. Do not label them as 2026 calendar totals. The judiciary's [bankruptcy filings statistics page](https://www.uscourts.gov/data-news/reports/statistical-reports/bankruptcy-filings-statistics) explains that filings are reported quarterly for each 12-month period ending March 31, June 30, September 30, and December 31, with county data in Report F-5A.

From 2022 to 2026, total filings grew by 237,301, or 61.8%, by our calculation. The starting point sits near a historic low, which makes the percentage look large.

## Year-Over-Year Growth Rates

Growth has not been even from year to year. Here are the annual changes in total filings, calculated from the published totals.

| Year Ending Sept. 30 | Total Filings | Change From Prior Year |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 2023 | 433,658 | +13.0% |
| 2024 | 504,112 | +16.2% |
| 2025 | 557,376 | +10.6% |
| 2026 | 621,111 | +11.4% |

The fastest growth came in the year to September 2024. Growth then slowed in 2025 before picking up slightly in 2026. These bankruptcy statistics show a steady climb, not an accelerating one.

## How Today Compares With the Peak

The release puts the rise in context. Filings fell from a high of nearly 1.6 million in September 2010 to a low of 380,634 in June 2022. They have risen each quarter since, but remain far lower than historical highs.

So two statements are true at once. Bankruptcy filings have climbed for four straight years. And they are still well under half of the 2010 peak.

Both facts belong in any honest summary of bankruptcy statistics. Quoting only the growth rate makes the trend sound like a crisis. Quoting only the 2010 comparison hides a steady four-year climb.

## Bankruptcy Filings by Chapter

The release also splits each year's filings by chapter. The chapter tells you what kind of relief the filer is seeking.

| Year Ending Sept. 30 | Chapter 7 | Chapter 11 | Chapter 12 | Chapter 13 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2022 | 229,703 | 4,762 | 182 | 149,077 |
| 2023 | 248,680 | 6,473 | 142 | 178,214 |
| 2024 | 298,644 | 9,012 | 202 | 195,971 |
| 2025 | 344,825 | 8,937 | 293 | 203,118 |
| 2026 | 391,386 | 9,986 | 361 | 219,116 |

The four chapters in 2026 add up to 620,849, which is 262 short of the total. The release does not break out the remainder, which falls under other chapters such as Chapter 15.

### Chapter 7

Chapter 7 made up about 63% of 2026 filings (391,386 divided by 621,111). It is the liquidation chapter. The national count mixes business and consumer cases, so it is not a consumer-only total.

Chapter 7 added 46,561 cases in one year. That is the largest numeric increase of any chapter.

### Chapter 13

Chapter 13 recorded 219,116 filings, about 35% of the total. It lets individuals with regular income repay debts under a court-approved plan, subject to eligibility rules.

A filing count does not show how many plans were completed. Filed, dismissed, discharged, and pending cases are separate measures.

### Chapter 11

Chapter 11 filings reached 9,986. The chapter covers business reorganizations and some individual cases. It is not a count of major corporate bankruptcies, since one large company can file many related cases.

### Chapter 12

Chapter 12 recorded 361 filings. It serves qualifying family farmers and fishermen. Its small size means modest changes produce large percentages: the move from 293 to 361 is a 23% rise on just 68 cases.

## Business Versus Non-Business Cases

The courts also classify each case as business or non-business, based on the type of debt involved.

| Case Type | 2026 Filings | Share of Total | Change From 2025 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Non-business | 594,057 | 95.6% | +11.4% |
| Business | 27,054 | 4.4% | +12.5% |

Business filings grew a little faster than the total. Even so, they remain under 5% of all cases.

These are court classifications. They do not capture all business distress. A sole owner's debts may appear in a non-business case, and many struggling companies close without filing at all.

## How the Chapter Mix Has Shifted

The balance between Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 has moved over five years. By our calculation from the published counts:

| Year Ending Sept. 30 | Chapter 7 Share | Chapter 13 Share |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 2022 | 59.8% | 38.8% |
| 2023 | 57.3% | 41.1% |
| 2024 | 59.2% | 38.9% |
| 2025 | 61.9% | 36.4% |
| 2026 | 63.0% | 35.3% |

Chapter 7 has gained share for three straight years, while Chapter 13 has lost it. The court data do not say why. Chapter choice depends on income, assets, eligibility rules, and legal advice, so any explanation needs research beyond these bankruptcy statistics.

Business filings changed even more. They more than doubled, from 13,125 in 2022 to 27,054 in 2026, a rise of about 106%. Non-business filings grew about 60% over the same span. Chapter 11 filings also more than doubled, from 4,762 to 9,986.

## Filed, Terminated, and Pending Cases

The judiciary's [Table F for the year ending September 30, 2026](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/bf_f_0930.2026.pdf) reports more than new filings.

| Measure | Year to Sept. 2025 | Year to Sept. 2026 | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Filed | 557,376 | 621,111 | +11.4% |
| Terminated | 536,590 | 556,601 | +3.7% |
| Pending | 687,517 | 749,734 | +9.0% |

Filings grew three times faster than terminations, so the pending caseload rose. A growing inventory does not prove courts are slower on its own. Case mix, complexity, and repayment plans that run for years all add to the pending count.

## Bankruptcy Statistics by Circuit

Table F also breaks filings down by federal circuit. The Eleventh Circuit, which covers Alabama, Florida, and Georgia, had the most filings in the year to September 2026.

| Circuit | 2025 Filings | 2026 Filings | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 11th | 92,746 | 108,062 | +16.5% |
| 9th | 95,430 | 106,393 | +11.5% |
| 6th | 80,417 | 88,130 | +9.6% |
| 5th | 55,448 | 65,058 | +17.3% |
| 7th | 54,678 | 57,485 | +5.1% |
| 4th | 43,528 | 50,519 | +16.1% |

The Fifth, Eleventh, and Fourth Circuits grew fastest, all above 16%. The Second Circuit grew just 3.6%.

Table F also shows district-level swings. Maryland filings rose 17.3% to 14,113, and New Jersey reached 15,025, up 8.9%. In North Carolina, the Western District rose 29.0% and the Middle District 28.7%. Delaware went the other way, falling 12.4% to 2,129.

Raw circuit totals mostly reflect population. A fair comparison would divide filings by residents or households in the same area and period. This page does not rank circuits by rate, because that would need population data matched to court boundaries.

## Why Filings Are Rising

The court data show that filings increased. They do not explain why.

Interest rates, household debt, business conditions, and access to credit can all affect bankruptcy activity. Assigning a share of the rise to any one factor would need separate research. The court release itself makes no causal claim.

## What Bankruptcy Statistics Do Not Show

National totals cannot tell you:

- **People Affected:** how many individuals joint cases cover.
- **Debt Discharged:** the total dollar amount wiped out.
- **Creditor Recovery:** how much creditors received.
- **Plan Completion:** how many Chapter 13 plans finished.
- **Unfiled Failures:** businesses that closed without filing.
- **Case Outcomes:** whether a given filing ended in discharge or dismissal.

A filing starts a case. It does not guarantee any particular result.

## How to Read Bankruptcy Percentages

Good bankruptcy statistics always report the count beside the percentage. Chapter 12's 23% rise added 68 cases. Chapter 7's 13.5% rise added 46,561. The smaller percentage describes a far bigger change.

The same caution applies across legal data. Like [car accident statistics](/legal/car-accident-statistics/), bankruptcy counts can rise sharply from an unusual low point. And like the count of [how many lawyers are in the US](/legal/how-many-lawyers-in-the-us/), they measure a defined legal status, not every person in a situation. ## Common Mistakes With Bankruptcy Data

Bankruptcy statistics are easy to misquote. These are the errors we see most often in pages that rank for the topic.

1. **Calendar labels on fiscal data.** The court year ends September 30, so "621,111 bankruptcies in 2026" is not accurate. Say "in the 12 months ending September 30, 2026."
2. **Mixing quarterly releases.** The courts publish rolling 12-month totals every quarter. A June total and a September total overlap by nine months and should never be added.
3. **Treating cases as people.** One joint filing covers two spouses. One corporate collapse can generate dozens of related cases.
4. **Reading Chapter 11 as big-company failures.** Chapter 11 includes small businesses and some individuals, not only large corporations.
5. **Ranking states by raw totals.** Large states always lead raw rankings, which says more about population than financial stress.

Avoiding these mistakes keeps a figure tied to what the court record measures.

## How to Use These Numbers

Different readers need different slices of the data.

- **Researchers and journalists:** Quote the 12-month period and the source table every time. Use the quarterly series to track direction, and compare like periods only.
- **Lenders and analysts:** Watch the business series and Chapter 11 separately, since they move differently from consumer filings.
- **Lawyers and law firms:** District tables show where caseloads are rising fastest, which can matter for staffing and practice planning.
- **Students:** Start with the five-year table, then read the chapter and circuit tables to see how the national total breaks down.

For local detail, the courts publish county figures in Report F-5A. Pair those counts with population data from the same year before you compare places.
