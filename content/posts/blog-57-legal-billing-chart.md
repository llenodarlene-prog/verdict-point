# Legal Billing Chart for Minutes and Tenths of an Hour

A legal billing chart converts recorded minutes into the time units used for an invoice. In a six-minute system, one unit equals 0.1 hour. However, exact elapsed time and rounded billing time describe different quantities.

For example, 15 minutes equals exactly 0.25 hour. An upward six-minute conversion produces 0.3 hour. Knowing which measure appears on the statement matters before multiplying it by an hourly rate.

The reference below explains that arithmetic and shows its limits. It also separates a conversion convention from the rules governing a particular engagement. All dollar examples are hypothetical, rather than market rates or actual client charges.

## Key Takeaways

- **Unit Size:** One tenth of an hour equals six minutes, while 0.5 hour equals 30 minutes.
- **Rounding Method:** Upward six-minute rounding differs from dividing minutes by 60 without rounding.
- **Billing Permission:** A correct conversion does not establish that a task or charge is permissible.
- **Small Tasks:** Check whether the applicable instructions require aggregation before converting individual entries.

## Quick Reference for Six Minute Increments

The following table uses upward increments for positive whole-minute durations. Its ranges match the [Northern District of California's CJA conversion chart](https://cand.uscourts.gov/attorneys/criminal-justice-act-cja/billing-increment-chart-minutes-tenths-hour). This court reference concerns Criminal Justice Act billing, rather than every private engagement.

Use the left column to locate the recorded duration. The final column shows the minutes represented by the converted amount.

| Recorded Minutes | Converted Hours | Minutes Represented |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1–6 | 0.1 | 6 |
| 7–12 | 0.2 | 12 |
| 13–18 | 0.3 | 18 |
| 19–24 | 0.4 | 24 |
| 25–30 | 0.5 | 30 |
| 31–36 | 0.6 | 36 |
| 37–42 | 0.7 | 42 |
| 43–48 | 0.8 | 48 |
| 49–54 | 0.9 | 54 |
| 55–60 | 1.0 | 60 |

The Florida Justice Administrative Commission chart independently lists the same minute ranges and tenths. Neither reference makes an unperformed task billable. For this calculation, zero recorded minutes produces zero hours.

## Exact Decimal Hours and Rounded Hours Differ

Exact decimal conversion divides elapsed minutes by 60. Upward six-minute rounding first identifies how many six-minute units cover the duration. Confusing those operations can change both the displayed time and the resulting fee.

These formulas describe the difference without selecting a billing policy.

- **Exact Hours:** Divide actual minutes by 60. For 15 minutes, calculate 15 ÷ 60 = 0.25.
- **Upward Units:** Divide minutes by six, then round that result upward to a whole number.
- **Converted Hours:** Multiply those upward units by 0.1. For 15 minutes, three units produce 0.3 hour.

At an exact boundary, the upward calculation adds no extra unit. Twelve minutes produces two units, or 0.2 hour. Thirteen minutes produces three units, or 0.3 hour, under this convention.

Preserve seconds when the original record contains them. A whole-minute chart does not explain how a system handles six minutes and one second. Confirm that setting rather than silently discarding the extra second or applying an additional minute-rounding step.

The formulas also clarify decimal notation. An entry of 0.30 means three tenths of an hour, or 18 minutes. It does not mean 30 minutes. Likewise, 1.30 decimal hours equals 78 minutes, not one hour and 30 minutes.

## Extend the Calculation Beyond One Hour

The same six-minute unit works for durations above 60 minutes. Keep the full duration in minutes, calculate the required units, and convert those units into hours. This avoids mixing clock notation with decimal notation.

The following examples are arithmetic illustrations using upward six-minute increments.

| Recorded Duration | Exact Decimal Hours | Upward Converted Hours |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 minutes | 1.00 | 1.0 |
| 61 minutes | 61 ÷ 60 | 1.1 |
| 75 minutes | 1.25 | 1.3 |
| 90 minutes | 1.50 | 1.5 |
| 125 minutes | 125 ÷ 60 | 2.1 |

For 75 minutes, dividing by six produces 12.5 units. Rounding upward produces 13 units, which equals 1.3 hours. For 125 minutes, the result is 21 units, or 2.1 hours.

Do not restart rounding merely because the clock passes an hour. Apply the agreed method to the appropriate recorded interval. Separately converting fragments can produce a different total from converting the complete duration once.

## Turn Converted Time into a Hypothetical Fee

For a time-based charge, multiply the applicable hourly rate by the approved hours. The District of New Hampshire's CJA explanation expressly describes upward tenths. Its instructions remain specific to that court's stated context.

Assume a hypothetical hourly rate of $240 for the following worksheet. Also assume the governing instructions permit each listed conversion. These invented amounts illustrate multiplication, rather than approved charges or typical legal prices.

| Illustrative Task | Recorded Minutes and Converted Hours | Hypothetical Fee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Document review | 17 minutes → 0.3 hour | $72 |
| Substantive client call | 24 minutes → 0.4 hour | $96 |
| Drafting revisions | 49 minutes → 0.9 hour | $216 |
| Total | 90 minutes recorded; 1.6 hours converted | $384 |

The fee calculation is $240 × 1.6 = $384. The recorded durations total exactly 90 minutes, or 1.5 elapsed hours. Separate upward rounding produces 1.6 converted hours, so the two totals differ.

That difference makes the unit of rounding important. Converting the combined 90-minute duration once would produce 1.5 hours and $360. Neither calculation establishes which method the engagement permits. Resolve that question before preparing the invoice.

If different professionals have different rates, calculate their lines separately. A single total of hours cannot establish the fee without the applicable rate allocation. Keep expenses outside this time calculation unless the agreement specifically explains their treatment.

### Different Rates Need Separate Lines

Consider a second hypothetical worksheet with two professionals. Assume one has an approved 0.4-hour entry at $250 per hour. Assume another has an approved 0.6-hour entry at $150 per hour. Their respective fees equal $100 and $90, producing a $190 total.

The combined time equals one hour, but neither individual hourly rate describes the combined charge. Multiplying that hour by $250 would produce $250, which overstates this hypothetical total by $60. Preserve each professional's rate and approved hours rather than selecting one rate for convenience.

### Changed Rates Need the Applicable Date

If a rate changes, identify which service dates the revised rate covers. Keep the old and new terms available during review. A current rate in the system does not establish the amount applicable to earlier work.

## Small Tasks Need More Than a Lookup

A legal billing chart does not decide whether several short tasks require aggregation. Separate upward conversions can produce more units than a combined conversion. The applicable instructions determine how the timekeeper should handle that difference.

The [Ninth Circuit CJA Compensability Handbook](https://idp.uscourts.gov/Content_Fetcher/index.cfml/Ninth_Circuit_CJA_Compensability_Handbook_4121.pdf?Content_ID=4121), approved April 20, 2022, addresses this directly. It instructs aggregation for tasks taking less than 0.1 hour. Its example combines two three-minute client calls into no more than 0.1 hour total.

Separately converting those calls would produce 0.2 hour, representing 12 minutes. Combining their six actual minutes produces 0.1 hour. That calculation explains why applying a chart independently to every interaction can conflict with specific billing instructions.

The handbook concerns Ninth Circuit CJA compensation. It does not establish a universal private-client aggregation rule. Check the relevant court requirements, client terms, and governing professional rules for the actual matter.

Keep enough detail to show the underlying activities even when instructions require aggregation. Combining time for a permitted calculation differs from hiding unrelated work behind an unclear description. Ask the responsible lawyer how to preserve the required detail.

## Check the Agreement and Governing Rules

Arithmetic comes after identifying the applicable billing basis. The lawyer needs to determine which work, rates, units, and instructions govern the matter. A spreadsheet default cannot supply those decisions.

Fee rules come from the jurisdiction that governs the engagement. California's Rule 1.5, for example, bars agreements for, or collection of, an unconscionable or illegal fee. Check the rules that apply to the particular representation.

The ABA's [Formal Opinion 93-379](https://www.americanbar.org/books/ecd/chapter/219943/) discusses time-based billing and minimum increments. The opinion says a lawyer may not bill more time than the lawyer spends, except to the extent of rounding up to minimum time periods such as one-tenth of an hour. It should not replace checking current governing requirements for the particular representation.

Before implementing a conversion policy, resolve these practical questions.

- **Applicable Instructions:** Which engagement terms, client guidelines, and court requirements govern this matter?
- **Recorded Precision:** Will timekeepers preserve seconds, whole minutes, or another original measurement?
- **Conversion Point:** Does conversion occur per task, permitted task group, or another defined interval?
- **Exceptions:** Who reviews uncertain billability, overlapping time, disputed entries, and unusual circumstances?

Also distinguish the charge from the funds used to pay it. The terms governing an [advance payment and remaining retainer balance](/law-firms/retainer-fee/) raise separate account questions. Correct time arithmetic alone does not establish permission to transfer client funds.

## Keep a Record That Explains the Calculation

Store the original duration alongside any converted entry. This lets a reviewer reproduce the calculation and identify where an adjustment occurred. Keep the description specific enough to connect the time with the actual matter.

As an editorial workflow recommendation, use the following sequence when reviewing entries.

- **Identify the Matter:** Confirm the client, assignment, service date, and professional who performed the work.
- **Check the Activity:** Describe the actual task and flag uncertain billability for the responsible lawyer.
- **Preserve Elapsed Time:** Retain the original duration before applying the documented conversion method.
- **Apply the Instructions:** Check any required aggregation, staffing limits, rate changes, or matter-specific exceptions.
- **Review the Result:** Recalculate the fee and preserve explanations for corrections before invoice approval.

A hypothetical description might read: reviewed supplier agreement termination provisions for scheduled negotiation. That description identifies an activity without inventing a client result. Avoid replacing accurate detail with a generic phrase merely to fit a template.

If the record contains uncertain or missing time, flag the gap. Do not fill it by selecting a convenient chart row. Ask for supporting records and document how the responsible professional resolves the entry.

## Test Timekeeping Settings Before Relying on Them

Evaluate a timekeeping system with known inputs before using its output in client invoices. The useful question concerns its actual calculation and review controls. A feature label or attractive dashboard does not prove the settings match the engagement.

For example, a firm assessing [Clio costs and workflow requirements](/legal-tech/clio-legal-software-total-cost/) should include conversion checks in that evaluation. Automatic capture raises the same question: [Smokeball's AutoTime](/legal-tech/smokeball-legal-software-time-capture/) turns tracked activity into entries that still need review before invoicing. This is a purchasing recommendation, not a claim about any specific plan's capabilities.

Use a small set of test entries with predetermined answers.

- **Boundary Checks:** Compare six, seven, 12, and 13 minutes under the intended upward convention.
- **Longer Durations:** Verify 60, 61, 75, and 90 minutes without confusing decimals with clock notation.
- **Task Grouping:** Compare two short entries with the permitted combined treatment under the relevant instructions.
- **Review Trail:** Check whether reviewers can locate original durations, adjustments, and the final approved calculation.

Also inspect exported files. Confirm whether a field contains elapsed minutes, decimal hours, or already converted billing units. Converting a value twice can change it, even when each individual operation appears familiar.

## Make the Reference Useful at Invoice Review

Keep the chart beside the applicable billing instructions, with the conversion method clearly identified. Ask reviewers to reproduce selected entries from their original durations. When a disagreement arises, identify whether it concerns time, method, rate, or permission to charge.

Those questions require different corrections. A misplaced decimal needs arithmetic repair. An uncertain task category needs professional review. A missing record needs evidence rather than a larger assumed duration.

Use the legal billing chart as a calculation aid within that process. Preserve the original time, apply the correct instructions, and resolve exceptions before sending the invoice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How Many Minutes Equal 0.1 Hour?

Exactly six minutes equal 0.1 hour. The legal billing chart maps positive durations up to six minutes into that unit.

### Does 0.30 Mean 30 Minutes?

No, 0.30 decimal hours equals 18 minutes. Thirty minutes equals 0.5 hour.

### Is 15 Minutes Always Billed as 0.3 Hour?

Fifteen minutes equals exactly 0.25 hour, while the upward legal billing chart conversion produces 0.3. The applicable billing instructions determine the treatment.

### Can Every Short Call Receive a Separate Minimum?

Do not assume that from a legal billing chart. The cited Ninth Circuit CJA guidance specifically requires aggregation for short tasks.

### Does a Legal Billing Chart Prove a Fee Is Reasonable?

No, it verifies a conversion under a stated method. Billability, fee reasonableness, and governing terms require separate review.
