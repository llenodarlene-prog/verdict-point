# Legal Industry Statistics by Firm Size

The U.S. legal market is usually described with one large revenue number or a headline about the biggest firms. Neither shows how the industry is built. The official firm-size data points the other way: nearly three in four law firms have fewer than five employees, and more than 95% have fewer than 20.

That structure changes how legal industry statistics should be read. A national employment figure cannot describe a solo practice, and results from large firms cannot be applied to every local office. This page uses the U.S. Census Bureau's 2022 firm-size data, the latest federal pay figure, and current large-firm reporting, each labeled with its own year. All sources were checked on October 3, 2026.

## Key Statistics and Data

Each figure below describes a different part of the market. The year and the population matter as much as the number.

- **Number of Law Firms:** The Census Bureau counted 156,583 firms in Offices of Lawyers in 2022, operating 166,972 locations.
- **Most Firms Are Very Small:** 116,668 firms, or 74.5%, had fewer than five employees.
- **Under 20 Employees:** 149,187 firms, or 95.3%, had fewer than 20 employees.
- **Industry Employment:** Offices of Lawyers employed 1,078,148 people in 2022, including non-lawyer staff.
- **Industry Receipts:** Those firms reported $356.2 billion in receipts in 2022.
- **Large Firms Earn a Large Share:** The 203 firms with 500 or more employees, 0.13% of all firms, took in $124.4 billion, about 34.9% of receipts.
- **Lawyer Pay:** The median annual wage for lawyers was $159,670 in May 2025, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.
- **Large-Firm Profit Growth:** The average firm tracked by the Thomson Reuters Institute grew profit 13% in 2025, with worked rates up 7.3%.

## Start With What the Data Counts

Legal industry statistics count people, businesses, locations, and money. Those categories overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A fair comparison starts by naming the unit behind each figure.

Table: What common legal-market measures count {.compare}
| Measure | What It Counts | What It Should Not Be Called |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Licensed lawyers | People admitted to practice | Employees or law firms |
| Employed lawyers | Wage and salary workers in the occupation | All lawyers or partners |
| Firm | A business enterprise, however many offices it has | An office location |
| Establishment | One operating location | A separate law firm |
| Enterprise employment size | All employees across the enterprise | Lawyers in one office |
| Receipts | Business revenue for the year | Profit or lawyer pay |

The firm and establishment distinction matters most. A firm is the whole business. An establishment is one location. A firm with several offices counts once as a firm and several times as establishments, which is why the 2022 data shows 166,972 establishments for 156,583 firms.

Size bands in this data use employees across the whole enterprise. They do not count lawyers, partners, or billers. A 15-person firm could include attorneys, paralegals, administrators, and intake staff, so calling it a 15-lawyer firm would change what the source says.

![Embossed seal and red ribbon on an ivory official document](/assets/images/posts/legal-industry-statistics-firm-size/official-record-seal-1600.jpg "Official datasets come with definitions. Keep the source's own terms.")

### Why Other Sources Report Far More Law Firms

Commercial industry reports sometimes put the number of U.S. law firms above 400,000. The Census figure of 156,583 is lower because this dataset covers businesses with paid employees. Sole practitioners with no employees fall outside it. Both numbers can be right for what they count, but they cannot be mixed in the same calculation.

### Why the Data Is From 2022

The Census Bureau released this 2022 table on April 10, 2025, and it is the latest detailed firm-size release available. Structural data like this moves slowly, so it remains useful, but it should always carry its 2022 label.

## Most U.S. Law Firms Are Small Enterprises

The [Census Bureau's 2022 detailed-size workbook](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/susb/tables/2022/us_state_naics_detailedsizes_2022.xlsx) gives a direct view of Offices of Lawyers by enterprise size. The distribution is concentrated at the small end.

The table below lists the same counts with each band's share of all firms.

| Enterprise Employment Size | Firms | Share of All Firms |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Under 5 employees | 116,668 | 74.5% |
| 5 to 9 employees | 22,518 | 14.4% |
| 10 to 19 employees | 10,001 | 6.4% |
| 20 to 49 employees | 4,912 | 3.1% |
| 50 to 99 employees | 1,333 | 0.9% |
| 100 to 499 employees | 948 | 0.6% |
| 500 or more employees | 203 | 0.1% |

Bands above nine employees combine non-overlapping Census rows. For example, the 20 to 49 band adds the 20 to 24, 25 to 29, 30 to 34, 35 to 39, and 40 to 49 rows.

These legal industry statistics do not say that small firms handle small matters. They show only how many firms fall into each employment band. Practice area, client type, geography, and pricing still shape the economics within each group.

## A Few Large Firms Take a Large Share of Revenue

Firm counts tell one story. Employment and receipts tell another, and these are the legal industry statistics that most often surprise readers. The same Census table reports how many people each size group employs and how much revenue it takes in.

```chart
law-firm-share-firms-employment-receipts-2022
```

Firms with fewer than 20 employees make up 95.3% of all firms but 43.7% of employment and 31.7% of receipts. The 203 firms with 500 or more employees are 0.1% of firms, yet they account for 18.2% of employment and 34.9% of receipts. The industry is small by headcount of businesses and large by concentration of money.

Table: Firms, employment, and receipts by enterprise size, 2022 {.cost}
| Enterprise Size | Firms | Employees | Receipts |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Under 20 employees | 149,187 | 471,188 | $112.8 billion |
| 20 to 499 employees | 7,193 | 410,779 | $119.1 billion |
| 500 or more employees | 203 | 196,181 | $124.4 billion |
| All firms | 156,583 | 1,078,148 | $356.2 billion |

The three size groups take in roughly equal revenue, about a third each, from very different numbers of firms.

### What Average Receipts per Firm Show

Dividing receipts by firms gives a rough sense of scale in each band.

```chart
law-firm-receipts-per-firm-2022
```

The average firm with fewer than five employees reported about $420,000 in receipts, while the average across all firms was about $2.3 million. Firms with 500 or more employees averaged about $613 million each. These are averages, not medians, so a few large firms in any band pull the figure up.

### What Receipts Do Not Show

Receipts are revenue, not profit and not owner income. A firm with $420,000 in receipts still pays rent, staff, software, and insurance before anyone takes a draw. The figures on margins and partner income in [law firm profitability statistics](/law-firms/law-firm-profitability-statistics/) answer a different question from the one revenue answers.

## Employment Is Not the Lawyer Population

The 1,078,148 employees in Offices of Lawyers include everyone on the payroll: lawyers, paralegals, legal assistants, finance, operations, marketing, and technology staff. It is a count of legal sector employment at law firms, not a count of lawyers.

Occupation data answers the lawyer question. The [Bureau of Labor Statistics lawyer profile](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/legal/lawyers.htm) reports 863,700 lawyer jobs in 2025 and a median annual wage of $159,670 in May 2025. It projects 5% job growth from 2025 to 2035.

That wage is not the income of a typical firm owner. It covers wage and salary workers, and partner pay often comes through draws and profit shares. The gap also matters for technology planning. Headcount includes many roles that use case management, billing, and document systems, so the adoption figures in [legal technology statistics](/legal-tech/legal-technology-statistics/) and [AI in law firms statistics](/legal-tech/ai-in-law-firms-statistics/) should be matched to roles, not just lawyer counts.

## Large-Firm Results Are a Market Pulse, Not a Census

Reports on large and midsize firms show where one commercially important segment is heading. They cannot describe a market where 95.3% of firms have fewer than 20 employees.

The Thomson Reuters Institute's [2026 Report on the State of the US Legal Market](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/institute/reports/state-of-the-us-legal-market-2026) found that the average firm it tracks grew profit 13% in 2025 and raised worked rates 7.3%. It also noted that about 90% of legal spending still runs through hourly billing.

Those are strong results for the firms in that study. Applying the 13% figure to all 156,583 firms in the Census table would be a mistake, because the sources use different populations, methods, and years. The same care applies to rates and collections. The most relevant [law firm billing statistics](/law-firms/law-firm-billing-statistics/) depend on which kind of firm is being measured.

## Firm Size Changes the Operating Model

The Census distribution explains why one industry-wide benchmark is rarely enough. A four-person practice and a multi-office enterprise do not carry the same staffing, technology, or management structure, so the same legal industry statistics mean different things to each.

### Smaller Firms Concentrate Responsibilities

In a small firm, lawyers often handle intake, client communication, billing, hiring, and marketing themselves. Cash costs can look lean because owner time absorbs work that a larger firm assigns to staff or vendors. That makes cost comparisons hard. A lower payroll line does not mean the work disappeared. It may sit with partners whose time is never recorded.

With average receipts of about $420,000 for firms under five employees, there is also little room for dedicated specialists. One software subscription or one hire is a meaningful share of revenue, which is why small firms weigh each purchase so closely.

### Larger Firms Add Coordination Costs

As firms grow, they can fund dedicated teams for finance, pricing, business development, information security, and operations. Specialization adds capacity and control, but it also adds coordination across people, systems, and offices.

Billing shows the difference. A small firm may run one compact process managed by a few people. A large enterprise may need approval rules, negotiated rates, invoice review, and e-billing formats across thousands of matters. Neither model is better. They are built for different volumes of work.

## How to Compare Legal-Market Numbers Without Mixing Them

Useful legal industry statistics do not all need the same method. The reader only needs to keep unlike measures apart and say what each one adds.

Table: Six checks before comparing legal-market numbers {.checklist}
| Check | Question to Ask |
| --- | --- |
| Name the population | Does this count firms, locations, employees, or lawyers? |
| Keep the reporting year | Is a 2022 count being presented as current? |
| Define the size measure | Is size based on employees, lawyers, or revenue? |
| Separate structure from momentum | Is this the market's shape or one segment's recent trend? |
| Keep pay, revenue, and profit apart | Is a wage being read as owner income? |
| Preserve the source's limits | Are exclusions and coverage rules stated? |

Following these checks makes the numbers less striking and more useful. It also keeps a benchmark built for one segment from becoming an unrealistic target for another.

## What the Firm-Size Data Means for Planning

The strongest conclusion is structural. A small number of very large enterprises sit beside a much larger population of small ones, and each third of industry revenue comes from a very different kind of firm.

That should shape how legal industry statistics are used in 2026 and 2027. A technology budget, pay comparison, or profit target should be matched to a firm of similar size. A large-firm figure can still be useful, but it should be labeled as evidence about that segment, not the whole market.

The practical approach is to use the 2022 Census data for market structure and newer sources for pay, demand, and pricing. That keeps the picture current without pretending every measure was collected in the same year. When the Census Bureau releases its next firm-size table, the counts here should be replaced and the comparison rerun, because the shares matter more than any single year.
