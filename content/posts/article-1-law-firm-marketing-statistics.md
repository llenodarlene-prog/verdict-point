# Law Firm Marketing Statistics: What the Numbers Mean for 2027 Budgets

A law firm marketing budget becomes easier to defend when every number has a job. That matters even more as firms move from late 2026 into 2027, because older benchmarks can still look current when they are repeated without context.

Current law firm marketing statistics point to three realities: firms still invest in websites, referrals, reviews, social media, and search; paid search remains costly; and intake can still break the link between a generated lead and a signed matter.

## Key Statistics and Data

These figures cover channel use, paid search costs, intake response, budget direction, and lead sources. They should be read as separate measures rather than one combined benchmark.

- Website Investment: About 85% of firms in Clio’s 2025 marketing chart invested in websites, while roughly 82% invested in referrals.  
- **Reviews and Social Media:** About 63% invested in online reviews and 54% in social media, while roughly 46% invested in search/SEO.  
- **Paid Search Cost:** WordStream and LocaliQ’s 2026 U.S. search-advertising benchmark reports $9.87 cost per click and $131.63 cost per lead for Attorneys & Legal Services.  
- **Phone Intake Gap:** Clio’s 2024 500-firm mystery-shopper study found that 40% answered phone calls and 48% were effectively unreachable by phone.  
- **Email Intake Gap:** Only 33% of firms in the same study responded to email inquiries.  
- **Budget Growth:** In the 2025 Legal Marketing Association and Above the Law survey, 54% of senior marketing and business-development respondents reported inflation-adjusted budget increases, while 14% reported decreases.  
- **Referral Dependence:** Clio’s 2025 solo and small-firm analysis found that 59% named referrals as their highest source of leads, compared with 27% of larger firms.

The first step is to separate the numbers by what they measure. A statistic can be accurate and still be the wrong number for the decision at hand.

## Websites, Referrals, Reviews, and Search Still Matter

Clio’s 2025 Legal Trends Report shows a clear pattern in where firms say they invest marketing attention. The full report is available here: https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/read-online/ About 85% invested in websites, 82% in referrals, 63% in online reviews, 54% in social media, and roughly 46% in search or SEO.

That does not prove firms are cutting total marketing spend. Instead, it shows how important the digital research path has become around the client decision.

For budget planning, a website should be treated as part of the acquisition system, not just a design project.

That is also why law firm website statistics (https://verdictpoint.org/legal-marketing/law-firm-website-statistics/) should separate visits from inquiries. Traffic can grow without improving the number of qualified prospects who contact the firm.

## Paid Search Is Expensive, but Cost per Lead Is Not Cost per Client

WordStream and LocaliQ’s 2026 U.S. search-advertising benchmark gives law firms a current reference point for paid acquisition. The methodology and current industry table are available here: https://www.wordstream.com/blog/2026-google-ads-benchmarks The study covers 13,474 U.S.-based search campaigns measured from April 2025 through March 2026.

For Attorneys & Legal Services, the reported benchmark figures were $9.87 per click, $131.63 per lead, and a 5.87% click-through rate. The category had the highest reported CPC and CPL among the industries measured.

However, a lead is not a signed matter. A $131.63 CPL can be attractive in one practice area and poor in another, depending on lead quality, response time, consultation rates, signing rates, and expected collected revenue.

For example, a $10,000 paid-search test divided by a $131.63 benchmark CPL produces about 76 leads. That is simple arithmetic, not a forecast.

The table stops before signed clients for a reason. There is no reliable universal legal lead-to-client rate that should be inserted into every firm’s plan.

To judge whether paid search is working, law firm client acquisition statistics (https://verdictpoint.org/law-firms/law-firm-client-acquisition-statistics/) need to follow the lead through qualification, consultation, signing, and collected revenue rather than stopping at CPL.

## Intake Can Waste an Otherwise Good Marketing Budget

Some of the most useful marketing evidence comes from client-intake research rather than ad platforms.

In 2024, Clio commissioned a third-party mystery-shopper study involving 500 law firms. Only 40% answered the phone, and 33% responded to email inquiries. Including firms that returned a call, 52% were reachable by phone, which means 48% were effectively unreachable in the study.

The same study found that only 30% of shoppers could easily understand the hiring process from a firm’s website, while only 14% could find pricing information.

Not every practice area should publish prices, and the study does not prove that pricing pages improve conversion. Still, the findings show how quickly marketing spend can lose value when prospects cannot reach the firm or understand the next step.

That makes intake part of the marketing budget.  
A firm should therefore measure response time, contact rate, consultation booking, show rate, signed matters, and reasons leads do not move forward. They help show whether the next dollar belongs in media, staffing, intake software, training, or follow-up.

## Firm Size Changes the Marketing Model

A solo firm and a 100-lawyer firm can spend very different amounts and still make sensible decisions.

Clio’s 2025 solo and small-firm analysis found that 59% of those firms named referrals as their highest source of leads, compared with 27% of larger firms. The ABA’s 2024 technology research also shows that solos and small firms often do more marketing work themselves.

Among solo attorneys, 76% personally managed or created content for the firm website, while 63% of small-firm attorneys did so. That means a smaller firm may show a low cash marketing expense while still using a large amount of attorney time.

Larger firms are more likely to carry marketing labor through payroll, internal teams, or outside agencies.

The 2025 Legal Marketing Association and Above the Law survey adds another angle. The report is available through LMA’s research library: https://www.legalmarketing.org/Advocacy/Industry-Research Among nearly 100 senior marketing and business-development leaders, 44% said budgets had increased slightly and 10% said they had increased significantly after adjusting for inflation. In contrast, 9% reported a slight decrease and 5% a significant decrease.

## Higher Marketing Spend Does Not Automatically Cause Higher Profit

Clio’s 2024 Legal Trends research found that firms with above-average utilization spent 41% more on marketing and 12% more on software than the comparison group. Those firms were also 21% more profitable.

Higher-performing firms may have more capacity to invest. They may also have stronger intake, a better market position, different practice areas, or better management. Therefore, the finding should not be read as an instruction to raise marketing spend by 41%.

For one firm, that constraint may be paid search. For another, it may be website clarity, review generation, CRM discipline, intake capacity, or content that supports referrals and organic discovery.

## Build a Law Firm Marketing Budget From the Funnel Backward

A useful budget begins with the type of work the firm wants and then works backward to the channels and systems needed to produce it.

### 1. Define the Matters the Firm Wants More Of

Start with the practice area, matter type, geography, capacity, and expected economics. This keeps the budget tied to work the firm can actually handle.

A firm that cannot take more cases in a practice area should not spend heavily to generate them.

### 2. Separate Leads From Qualified Opportunities

A lead can mean many things. It may be a form submission, a phone call, spam, an existing client, an opposing party, a job applicant, or a real prospect.

For that reason, define a qualified lead separately. The firm should be able to explain why a lead counts before it uses the number in a budget report.

### 3. Measure the Stages After the Lead

Once the definitions are set, the firm needs a short list of funnel stages that can be tracked consistently.

- **Leads:** All inquiries that meet the firm’s tracking definition.  
- **Qualified Leads:** Prospects that fit the practice area, geography, and case criteria.  
- **Successful Contact:** Qualified prospects the firm actually reaches.  
- **Consultations Booked:** Qualified prospects who schedule the next step.  
- **Consultations Completed:** Booked prospects who attend.  
- **Matters Signed:** Prospects who become clients.  
- **Collected Revenue:** Revenue actually received from signed work.  
- **Marketing Cost:** Media, tools, agencies, payroll, and other tracked acquisition costs.

These stages turn cost per lead into a more useful cost per signed matter.

### 4. Budget for Intake Capacity

If the firm cannot answer calls, respond to email, or follow up on qualified inquiries, more lead volume may increase waste.

Before raising media spend, check whether intake capacity is the real bottleneck.

### 5. Compare Channels on the Same Outcome

Paid search can look expensive beside referrals because one has an obvious media bill and the other does not. However, referrals still depend on reputation, relationships, client experience, and time.

Organic search has a similar measurement problem. The traffic may look “free,” while content production, technical work, authority building, and intake costs sit elsewhere.

That is why law firm SEO statistics (https://verdictpoint.org/legal-marketing/law-firm-seo-statistics/) are most useful when search visibility is connected to qualified inquiries and signed work.

## What Should a Law Firm Marketing Dashboard Show?

A good dashboard should answer three questions without forcing leadership to decode dozens of metrics.

### Are We Creating Enough Demand?

Start with measures that show whether the firm is generating attention and qualified interest.

- **Qualified Traffic:** Visits from people who fit the target market.  
- **Calls and Forms:** Direct inquiries that can be tied to a source.  
- **Referrals:** New prospects sent by clients, lawyers, or other partners.  
- **Review Activity:** New reviews and rating changes where relevant.  
- **Branded Search:** Searches that indicate direct awareness of the firm.  
- **Qualified Leads:** Inquiries that meet the firm’s case criteria.

### Are We Converting the Demand We Already Have?

Next, track whether the intake process moves good prospects forward.

- **Response Time:** How quickly the firm replies to new inquiries.  
- **Contact Rate:** The share of qualified leads the firm reaches.  
- **Consultations Booked:** Qualified leads that schedule.  
- **Show Rate:** Booked prospects who attend.  
- **Signed Matters:** Consultations that become clients.  
- **Loss Reasons:** Why qualified prospects do not move forward.

### Are the Economics Acceptable?

Finally, leadership needs the measures that connect marketing activity to money.

- **Spend by Channel:** Media, agency, technology, and other direct costs.  
- **Cost per Qualified Lead:** Spend divided by qualified opportunities.  
- **Cost per Consultation:** Spend divided by completed consultations.  
- **Cost per Signed Matter:** Spend divided by new clients.  
- **Collected Revenue:** Cash received from the matters acquired.  
- **Contribution Margin:** Revenue left after direct delivery costs, where the firm can measure it reliably.

The closer the metric gets to signed and collected work, the more useful it becomes for budget planning.

## There Is No Single Correct Law Firm Marketing Percentage

Many marketing guides suggest that firms should spend a fixed percentage of revenue. That can be useful as an internal guardrail, but it is not a universal benchmark.

Current law firm marketing statistics show meaningful differences in firm size, lead sources, paid acquisition costs, and operating models. A growth-stage consumer firm competing for high-value cases may reasonably spend very differently from a mature business-law practice built on institutional relationships.

A percentage can organize a budget. It cannot replace practice-area economics, capacity, intake performance, or the firm’s own cost per signed matter.

## What the 2026 Data Suggests for 2027 Law Firm Marketing Budgets

Taken together, the current evidence supports a more balanced way to plan.

First, websites, referrals, reviews, social media, and search remain major parts of how firms market themselves. Second, paid search is expensive, with legal services posting some of the highest benchmark acquisition costs in the 2026 WordStream and LocaliQ data. Third, intake is still a serious point of loss, which means more lead volume does not always create more clients.

Firm size also changes the economics. Smaller firms often depend more on referrals and attorney-led marketing work, while larger firms are more likely to carry dedicated marketing staff and outside support.

For 2027 planning, the goal should not be to copy another firm’s percentage. It should be to connect the full path from attention to signed and collected work, then invest where the firm’s own data shows the biggest constraint.

The numbers become much more useful once they are tied to that decision.
