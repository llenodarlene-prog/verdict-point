# Law School Acceptance Rates: 2026 Admissions Statistics

National law school acceptance rates fell in the 2025 cycle. AccessLex reports that the law school admission rate dropped four percentage points to 65%, the lowest level in any cycle since 2006. The 2026 cycle then closed with 82,487 applicants and 579,839 applications, according to the Law School Admission Council (LSAC).

Those two numbers answer different questions. Applicants are people. Applications are the files those people send to schools. Mixing them is the most common mistake on pages about law school acceptance rates, and it can make a national figure look far lower than it is.

This page keeps each measure separate, names the cycle behind every number, and shows how to compare one school's rate with another's. All figures were checked against the publishers' pages on November 10, 2026.

## Key Takeaways

- **National Rate Fell:** The 2025 law school admission rate was 65%, down four points and the lowest since 2006.
- **More Competition:** Applicants rose 18% in 2025, while admitted applicants rose 11% and matriculants 8%.
- **2026 Volume Grew Again:** LSAC counted 82,487 applicants, up 7.9%, who sent 579,839 applications, up 10.8%.
- **Seven Files per Person:** The 2026 totals work out to about seven applications for each applicant.
- **No 2026 National Rate Yet:** LSAC's cycle summary gives no admitted total, so a 2026 national rate cannot be calculated from it.

## The National Admission Rate Trend

AccessLex tracks the full admissions pipeline in its [Legal Education Data Deck](https://www.accesslex.org/research-and-data-tools-and-resources/legal-education-data-deck). Its spring 2026 edition reports 76,500 law school applicants in 2025, an increase of 11,700, or 18%, over 2024.

Admissions did not keep pace. Admitted applicants grew 11% and matriculants grew 8%. Because the pool of people grew faster than the number of offers, the share of applicants admitted fell.

| 2025 Cycle Measure | Reported Result |
|---|---:|
| Applicants | 76,500, up 18% |
| Admitted applicants | Up 11% |
| Matriculants | Up 8% |
| National admission rate | 65%, down 4 points |

AccessLex adds useful context. The rate had hovered around 69% for about five years before the 2025 drop. That makes 2025 a clear break from a stable period, not a small wobble.

The same deck reports that J.D. degrees awarded leveled off at 36,340 in 2025. Degrees reflect students who entered about three years earlier, so they lag behind application trends.

## What the 2026 Cycle Showed

LSAC closed the 2026 admission cycle in September. In its [September 2026 data update](https://www.lsac.org/podcast/keeping-data-september-2026), LSAC reported these totals:

| 2026 Cycle Measure | Total | Change From 2025 | Change From 2024 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Applicants | 82,487 | +7.9% | +27.4% |
| Applications | 579,839 | +10.8% | +35.5% |

Applications grew faster than applicants. On average, each person applied to more schools. That pattern often appears when candidates expect a tougher cycle and spread their bets.

LSAC also described who applied. Women made up 57.3% of the applicant pool and men 40%. Applicants from racially or ethnically minoritized backgrounds were 50.2% of the pool. First-generation applicants made up just over 28%.

Testing rose too. LSAC said the number of LSAT tests taken was up 13.3% over the previous year, and the number of people taking the test was up 11.1%.

## Applicants and Applications Are Different

The 2026 totals show why the denominator matters. Here is the arithmetic.

| Measure | 2026 Total | What It Counts |
|---|---:|---|
| Applicants | 82,487 | Unique people in the cycle |
| Applications | 579,839 | Files sent to individual schools |
| Applications per applicant | About 7.0 | 579,839 divided by 82,487 |

A national applicant admission rate divides admitted people by all people who applied. A school's acceptance rate divides offers by the applications that school received. One person can collect several offers, so the two rates can sit far apart.

Suppose you divided a national count of offers by 579,839 applications. You would get a per-application figure that looks much harsher than the 65% applicant rate. Neither number is wrong. They simply measure different things.

## How the 2025 and 2026 Figures Fit Together

The two publishers use the same underlying cycle data, but they round and present it differently. AccessLex gives a rounded 2025 count of 76,500 applicants. LSAC reports 82,487 applicants for 2026 and calls that a 7.9% increase over 2025.

You can test whether those statements agree. Dividing 82,487 by 1.079 gives roughly 76,450 applicants for 2025. That sits within rounding distance of the AccessLex figure, so the two sources tell a consistent story. Treat the 76,450 result as a check on the math, not as a new official count.

The comparison also shows the size of the two-year jump. LSAC says 2026 applicants were 27.4% above 2024. In plain terms, roughly 17,700 more people applied in 2026 than two cycles earlier.

| Cycle | Applicants | Source |
|---|---:|---|
| 2024 | About 64,800 | AccessLex (76,500 minus 11,700) |
| 2025 | About 76,500 | AccessLex, rounded |
| 2026 | 82,487 | LSAC, completed cycle |

The 2024 row is our subtraction from the AccessLex figures, and it is rounded. Use it to see the direction and size of the change, and cite the publishers for exact counts.

This is the backdrop for school-level law school acceptance rates in the next few cycles. Growth on this scale puts pressure on seats. Unless schools expand their entering classes at the same speed, more applicants usually means a lower share admitted, which is what the 2025 rate showed.

## How to Calculate a School's Acceptance Rate

The standard school-level formula is simple:

**Acceptance rate = offers of admission ÷ completed applications × 100**

Take a hypothetical school with 4,000 completed applications and 800 offers. Its acceptance rate is 20%.

That 20% says nothing about how many admitted students enrolled. Enrollment depends on yield.

**Yield = enrolled students ÷ offers of admission × 100**

If 240 of those 800 admitted applicants enroll, yield is 30%. Acceptance describes how selective a school is. Yield describes how many admitted candidates chose it.

These examples are illustrations, not data for any real school.

## Where to Find Comparable School Data

Law schools publish their own admissions profiles, and most report completed applications, offers, and first-year enrollment for each entering class. When you compare schools, pull every number from the same entering year.

For each school on your list, note:

- **Applications and Offers:** Record completed applications and offers so you can calculate the rate yourself.
- **Enrollment:** Note first-year enrollment, which shows yield and class size.
- **Entering Profile:** Write down the academic profile the school reports for that class.
- **Cost Terms:** Capture tuition, fees, and any conditions attached to scholarships.
- **Reporting Year:** Copy the entering year printed on the disclosure, so you never mix cycles.

School-published law school acceptance rates are only comparable when every school on your list reports the same entering year.

A low rate can come from heavy application volume, a small class, or both. Read the rate beside class size before you draw a conclusion.

## Why Early-Cycle Numbers Need Care

LSAC's [current volume summaries](https://www.lsac.org/data-research/data/current-volume-summaries-region-raceethnicity-gender-identity-lsat-score) are updated daily during each cycle. For the 2026-2027 cycle, the page currently says it is too early to reach conclusions about applicant volume.

That warning matters. A November comparison captures only the first wave of applications. Many candidates file later, so early growth rates can shift a great deal before a cycle closes.

LSAC's September update gave early signals for the next testing year. Tests administered in August were up about 7% over the prior August. September registrations were up about 5%, and October registrations appeared to be up about 3%. Those are testing indicators, not application totals, and they should be labeled that way.

## Acceptance Rate Versus Your Own Chances

A published rate is an outcome for a past pool of applicants. It is not your personal probability.

Your result depends on your academic record, written materials, timing, work history, and each school's review process. A 20% school-wide rate does not give every candidate a one-in-five chance. Strong candidates may face much better odds, and others much worse.

National law school acceptance rates also hide differences between groups. Any subgroup comparison needs a named population, a period, and a source. Blending rates from different years or databases can mislead.

## Why Ranking Pages Often Disagree

Pages that list law school acceptance rates can disagree for honest reasons:

1. They use different entering years.
2. One divides by all applications, another by completed applications.
3. A school corrected an earlier disclosure.
4. Full-time and part-time programs are combined differently.
5. One figure comes from the school and another from a third-party database.

The page's update date is not the admissions year. A page refreshed in 2026 may still show a 2024 entering class. Keep each rate tied to its source cycle.

## A Better Way to Compare Schools

Selectivity is only one signal. Use five measures together.

| Measure | Question It Answers |
|---|---|
| Acceptance rate | What share of completed applications received an offer? |
| Yield | What share of admitted applicants enrolled? |
| Class size | How many students entered? |
| Entering credentials | What academic profile did the class report? |
| Cost and outcomes | What did school cost, and what happened after graduation? |

This keeps a low acceptance rate from standing in for quality, value, or job results.

Career outcomes deserve their own research. The legal market is large and varied, as the [legal industry statistics by firm size](/legal/legal-industry-statistics-firm-size/) show, and practice areas differ widely in work and pay. A look at the main [types of lawyers](/legal/types-of-lawyers/) can help you test whether a school's strengths match the career you want.

## What These Numbers Cannot Tell You

National admissions data cannot tell you whether law school is right for you. They do not measure teaching quality, debt, or the value of a degree in your local market.

They also cannot predict the legal work you will see after graduation. Some graduates move into intellectual property, where the [patent litigation statistics](/legal/patent-litigation-statistics/) show thousands of new federal cases each year. Others move into family practice, where [divorce statistics](/legal/us-divorce-statistics/) describe the volume of cases in the courts. Admissions data say nothing about either path.

The 65% national rate describes the whole 2025 system. It should not be applied to any one school. Read law school acceptance rates as one input among several, never as a verdict on fit or value.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Is the Average Law School Acceptance Rate?

The national law school admission rate was 65% in the 2025 cycle, according to AccessLex. Individual schools can be far above or below that figure.

### How Many People Applied to Law School in 2026?

LSAC reported 82,487 applicants in the completed 2026 cycle, up 7.9% from 2025.

### How Many Applications Were Submitted in 2026?

Applicants submitted 579,839 applications in the 2026 cycle, about seven per person.

### Is an Application the Same as an Applicant?

No. One applicant can apply to many schools, so application totals are much larger than applicant totals.

### Does a Low Acceptance Rate Mean a Better School?

No. A low rate measures selectivity for one applicant pool. Cost, bar passage, and job outcomes need separate evidence.

### Why Did the National Rate Fall in 2025?

Applicants grew 18% while admitted applicants grew only 11%, so a smaller share of people received offers.
