# Document Management Software Requirements for Law Firms

Document management software should fit how a law firm creates, finds, controls, and transfers its working records. The purchasing question extends beyond storage capacity. Reviewers need to establish which documents the system holds, who can access them, and how the firm retrieves them later.

Start with the work your staff performs. A requirements list becomes useful when someone can demonstrate each important task. Ask for evidence using approved sample material rather than accepting a feature label as proof.

The checklist below offers a procurement framework for US law firms. It separates professional guidance from suggested evaluation tasks. It does not rank vendors, certify a product, or prescribe a universal retention schedule.

## Key Takeaways

- **Matter Access:** Test who can find, open, edit, share, and administer documents within the proposed configuration.
- **Working History:** Check versions, email attachments, and retrieval using representative sample records.
- **Recovery and Exit:** Ask vendors to demonstrate restoration and a usable export, with responsibilities documented.
- **Written Scope:** Confirm which capabilities, services, limits, and costs belong to the actual proposal.

## Define the Records and Workflows in Scope

Identify which records the proposed system will manage and which will remain elsewhere. Include working documents, signed copies, email, and relevant attachments in that discussion. Record who maintains the connection between the file and its matter.

For a general definition, [Interscan describes a DMS](https://www.interscanllc.com/resources/document-management/document-management-systems) as a system for organizing electronic documents and related content. Its explanation includes version control, access control, and retrieval. Those are evaluation areas, rather than proof that every product implements them adequately.

Also distinguish storing an agreement from managing its commitments. A business establishing [contract stages and operational ownership](/legal-business/contract-lifecycle-management/) needs decisions and handoffs beyond file storage. Document control can support that work without becoming the complete contract process.

If the project also involves litigation review, assess that scope separately. A firm considering [eDiscovery document-review requirements](/legal-tech/everlaw-ediscovery-document-review/) should identify its own relevant tasks and evidence. Do not assume one procurement exercise establishes suitability for every document-related workflow.

## Connect the Evaluation with Professional Responsibilities

[ABA Model Rule 1.6(c)](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_6_confidentiality_of_information/) requires reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized access and inadvertent or unauthorized disclosure. It concerns information relating to client representation. This is a model provision, so check the rules governing the firm's actual practice.

Comment 18 to that rule identifies factors relevant to reasonable safeguards. They include information sensitivity, disclosure likelihood, cost, implementation difficulty, and effects on representation. The assessment requires context rather than a vendor's blanket assurance.

Separately, Comment 8 to Model Rule 1.1 addresses keeping abreast of relevant technology's benefits and risks. Use qualified legal and technical reviewers when evaluating the proposed arrangement. The [legal technology statistics](/legal-tech/legal-technology-statistics/) show how common cloud tools and formal security policies are across firms. A procurement checklist supports their work without replacing their judgment.

Ask those reviewers to identify any applicable client instructions, contractual conditions, or other requirements before testing. Record the source of each requirement. This prevents an internal preference from appearing indistinguishable from a legal obligation.

## Turn Requirements into Observable Tests

A requirement should describe the needed behavior and the evidence that demonstrates it. Avoid relying on broad labels such as secure, searchable, or integrated. Ask what the proposed configuration does with the firm's actual types of work.

Use this matrix as a starting point. Its tests are editorial recommendations, rather than mandatory technical specifications.

| Requirement Area | Demonstration Task | Evidence to Record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Matter organization | File a sample document to the correct matter | Matter association and retrieval result |
| Restricted access | Search and open the sample using different roles | Authorized access and denied-access behavior |
| Version history | Save revisions and retrieve an earlier version | Version identity, author, and available history |
| Email handling | File a message and its sample attachment | Message, attachment, and matter relationship |
| Search | Find an approved sample by content and metadata | Search result, limitations, and role restrictions |
| Recovery | Restore a permitted test record | Restored content, scope, and elapsed recovery time |
| Export | Export the sample matter and inspect its contents | Usable files, metadata, omissions, and dependencies |

For each test, record the product version, plan, settings, and participant role. Save the result and any unresolved limitation. A demonstration in a different configuration may not establish what the firm would receive.

Give essential requirements a clear pass condition before scoring proposals. If a critical task fails, record that separately. A high score for optional convenience features should not conceal a failed essential requirement.

## Prepare a Safe and Representative Demonstration Pack

Build a small collection of fictional records that reflects the firm's working patterns. Include a document with revisions, a scanned sample, an email attachment, and two similarly named matters. Keep the material free of real client information unless an authorized assessment permits its use.

Give each vendor the same task instructions and expected outcomes. Explain which staff roles should participate. Ask the vendor to identify any prerequisite configuration before starting, rather than changing the test silently midway through.

Capture the following information during the demonstration.

- **Task Outcome:** State whether the participant completed the requested action and what the reviewer observed.
- **Configuration:** Record settings, permissions, add-ons, and integrations necessary for the demonstrated behavior.
- **Limitations:** Note unsupported file types, unavailable history, manual steps, and unanswered questions.
- **Follow-up Evidence:** Request written confirmation when the demonstration cannot establish an important requirement.

Allow actual users to perform selected tasks after the demonstration. Observe where they need assistance and what instructions resolve the problem. Record those observations without turning one short session into a guaranteed productivity claim.

## Examine Access Changes and the Review Trail

Test access across the different ways a person might encounter a document. Include ordinary navigation, search, shared links, and administrative functions where relevant. Ask which permissions govern each route and who can change them.

The [NIST small-business cybersecurity guide](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.1300.pdf), published in 2024, recommends restricting sensitive information to staff who need it. It also prioritizes multifactor authentication and questions whether unnecessary access is removed. This is general risk guidance, not a law-firm product certification.

For a proposed access workflow, ask reviewers to demonstrate these changes.

- **Create Access:** Add a fictional user with the intended ordinary role and confirm the assigned permissions.
- **Restrict a Matter:** Verify what that user can discover, open, edit, and share within the test scenario.
- **Change the Assignment:** Remove the user's matter access and repeat the relevant retrieval attempts.
- **Review Activity:** Locate the available record of changes and determine who can inspect or alter it.

Ask separately about active sessions, offline copies, downloaded files, and external recipients. Record what the system can control and what needs another process. Do not infer that removing an account also removes every copy already obtained.

For logging, identify the events recorded and how long the history remains available. Ask whether exports preserve those records and who administers retention. Do not assume that an audit-log label means every action is recorded or that records cannot change.

## Check Versions, Search, and Everyday Retrieval

A useful version test shows what happens when people revise the same working document. Ask participants to create successive revisions, locate an earlier one, and identify the current version. Record how the system handles conflicting edits under the proposed workflow.

Also test the relationship between an email and its attachment. Ask reviewers to retrieve the message, distinguish attachment revisions, and identify the related matter. Keep this separate from merely confirming that a file can upload.

For search, choose sample content whose correct result the reviewer already knows. Include similar names and a scanned document if those reflect the firm's work. Ask what preparation or text recognition the sample needs before content search can find it.

Repeat the search using a restricted role. Examine whether the result reveals document names or other information the user should not receive. Record the behavior and have the responsible reviewers assess its acceptability.

Avoid changing the requirement after seeing an attractive result. If a search needs a specific add-on, record that dependency. Obtain written confirmation of its scope and commercial terms before treating the requirement as satisfied.

## Separate Recovery from Retention Decisions

Recovery concerns restoring records after a problem, while retention concerns how long records remain and what happens afterward. Keep those questions separate during procurement. A history screen does not establish the complete recovery arrangement.

NIST's cited guide recommends regular backups and testing those backups. Turn that principle into a documented recovery discussion with the vendor and the firm's technical reviewers. Identify who performs restoration and what evidence the firm can obtain.

Ask for the restore scope, available recovery points, exclusions, support process, and expected timing. Use an approved test to examine the proposed arrangement. Record the observed result as a test result, rather than a guarantee for every incident.

For retention and deletion, have the responsible lawyer identify the applicable requirements before configuring automation. Ask how preservation instructions affect scheduled deletion. Avoid adopting one retention period across all records merely because the software offers a default.

Also identify who can approve destruction and how that decision is recorded. Review what happens to versions, attachments, exports, and recoverable copies. Do not equate deleting one visible document with verified deletion from every location.

## Test Export and Migration Before Committing

Document management software selection should include a usable exit test. Ask the vendor to export the approved sample matter, then inspect it outside the product. This tests the proposed output rather than relying on a promise that data belongs to the firm.

Use a written inventory to compare the original sample and the export.

- **File Contents:** Check that expected documents and attachments open in the formats the firm needs.
- **Context:** Identify which matter identifiers, dates, authors, and other metadata accompany the files.
- **History:** Confirm whether versions and activity records appear, remain available separately, or fall outside the export.
- **Dependencies:** Record any special viewer, paid service, administrator action, or additional license needed.

For migration, agree what the source contains and what the destination should receive. Test a small approved batch before transferring the complete collection. Reconcile expected records with results and investigate omissions rather than relying on a successful upload message.

Resolve the exit terms in writing before signing. Ask about timing, support charges, access after termination, and deletion confirmation. Have appropriate reviewers assess those terms alongside the technical demonstration.

## Compare the Full Cost of the Proposed Scope

Use the same scope when comparing vendor quotations. Identify user types, storage assumptions, required functionality, implementation work, and ongoing support. Record unknown amounts rather than entering zero simply to complete a comparison.

The following worksheet is entirely hypothetical. Assume 20 users, a $50 monthly license per user, and 12 charged months. Assume separate migration and training charges, with no overlap between the listed items.

| Illustrative Cost Item | Assumed Calculation | Hypothetical Amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annual user licenses | 20 × $50 × 12 months | $12,000 |
| Migration service | Assumed one-time charge | $4,000 |
| Initial training | Assumed one-time charge | $2,000 |
| First-year subtotal | Sum of the three listed items | $18,000 |

The subtotal includes only these assumptions. It excludes taxes, internal staff time, additional storage, integrations, and other unlisted items. It does not represent any vendor's pricing or a market benchmark.

For a real proposal, verify what each charge includes and when it applies. Separate recurring expenses from one-time work. Ask whether required capabilities change the plan, user minimum, renewal price, or implementation scope.

## Make the Selection and Rollout Reviewable

Collect the requirement matrix, demonstration results, written proposal, and unresolved questions in one decision record. Identify who accepts any remaining limitation. Revisit essential requirements if the proposed plan or configuration changes before signature.

For rollout, assign ownership of configuration, migration reconciliation, training, and support. Give users a clear way to report problems. Review the agreed acceptance tasks before expanding the deployment.

Choose document management software using that evidence and the firm's actual responsibilities. Confirm access, retrieval, recovery, and exit in the proposed arrangement. Keep the decision record available when the firm later changes its requirements or supplier.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Document Management Software Automatically Establish Compliance?

No, choosing document management software does not replace reviewing the firm's applicable duties and configuration. The cited ABA provisions require contextual professional assessment.

### Should Every Firm Use the Same Retention Period?

Do not adopt a universal period from this checklist. Have the responsible lawyer determine applicable requirements before configuring retention and deletion.

### Does Version History Prove That Recovery Will Work?

A version-history demonstration in document management software does not establish the complete recovery arrangement. Check the relevant restoration process and test evidence separately.

### Should a Vendor Demonstration Use Real Client Files?

Use approved fictional material for the suggested tests. Any proposed use of real information requires the firm's authorized assessment and safeguards.

### Are the Worksheet Prices Actual Vendor Rates?

No, all prices and quantities in that worksheet are hypothetical. Obtain a written document management software quotation covering the firm's required scope.
