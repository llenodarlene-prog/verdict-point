# Workplace Harassment Statistics: EEOC Charges and Outcomes

The EEOC received 37,397 charges alleging harassment in fiscal year 2025, up from 35,774 the year before. That is the highest count in the agency's table, which starts in fiscal 2010. Of those, 8,475 were Title VII sexual harassment charges.

Those are workplace harassment statistics about formal charges. A charge is an allegation filed with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC). It is not a finding that harassment happened, and most people who experience harassment never file one.

This page shows the latest EEOC data, explains how charges are resolved, and names what the counts leave out. We matched every figure to the EEOC's own tables and reports on November 13, 2026.

## Key Takeaways

- **Record Harassment Charges:** The EEOC received 37,397 harassment charges in FY2025, the most since its series began in FY2010.
- **About Four in Ten Charges:** Harassment charges equal about 42% of the 88,201 total charges, by our calculation.
- **Sexual Harassment:** Title VII sexual harassment charges reached 8,475 in FY2025.
- **Most Find No Cause:** About 61% of harassment charges resolved in FY2025 ended with a no-reasonable-cause finding.
- **Money Recovered:** Harassment charge resolutions produced $261.9 million in monetary benefits in FY2025.

## Harassment Charges by Year

The EEOC's [Table E2a on charges alleging harassment](https://www.eeoc.gov/sites/default/files/2026-03/Table_E2a._Charges_Alleging_Harassment_Charge_Receipts_and_Resolutions_by_Type_%28All_Statutes%29_FY_2010_-_FY_2025.xlsx) covers every statute the agency enforces. Fiscal years end September 30.

| Fiscal Year | Harassment Charges Received | Resolutions | Monetary Benefits |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2021 | 21,270 | 22,323 | $142.2 million |
| 2022 | 24,430 | 21,680 | $144.1 million |
| 2023 | 31,354 | 29,051 | $202.2 million |
| 2024 | 35,774 | 33,891 | $211.8 million |
| 2025 | 37,397 | 37,613 | $261.9 million |

Receipts climbed 76% from FY2021 to FY2025, by our calculation. FY2021 was the low point of the series, so part of that rise is a return from an unusual year. Even so, FY2025 is well above the roughly 27,000 charges a year the EEOC recorded through most of the 2010s.

The monetary benefits column excludes money won through litigation, according to the table's notes.

## Sexual Harassment Charges

Sexual harassment is one type of harassment charge. The EEOC tracks it separately in [Table E2c on Title VII sexual harassment](https://www.eeoc.gov/sites/default/files/2026-03/Table_E2c._Title_VII_Sexual_Harassment_Charge_Receipts_and_Resolutions_by_Type_of_Resolution_FY_2010_-_FY_2025.xlsx).

| Fiscal Year | Sexual Harassment Charges | Resolutions | Monetary Benefits |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2021 | 5,581 | 6,189 | $61.6 million |
| 2022 | 6,201 | 5,664 | $59.0 million |
| 2023 | 7,732 | 7,119 | $60.6 million |
| 2024 | 8,474 | 8,191 | $78.1 million |
| 2025 | 8,475 | 8,689 | $86.12 million |

Sexual harassment charges were flat in FY2025, up by a single charge. But benefits recovered rose about 10%. The count is also the highest in the table since FY2010, when the EEOC received 7,944 such charges.

## The 15-Year Trend

The EEOC's harassment table starts in FY2010. Charges hovered between about 26,000 and 28,000 a year through FY2019, then fell to a low of 21,270 in FY2021. They have risen every year since.

| Fiscal Year | Harassment Charges | Monetary Benefits |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 2010 | 27,356 | $118.7 million |
| 2016 | 28,216 | $125.5 million |
| 2019 | 26,221 | $139.6 million |
| 2021 | 21,270 | $142.2 million |
| 2025 | 37,397 | $261.9 million |

FY2025 is about 33% above FY2016, the highest year before the recent climb, by our calculation. Monetary benefits more than doubled from FY2010 to FY2025. Those dollar figures are not adjusted for inflation, so part of the rise reflects higher prices rather than larger recoveries.

These workplace harassment statistics describe the EEOC's charge intake. A change in how the agency takes or codes charges can move the series too, so compare several years before drawing a conclusion.

## How Harassment Charges Are Resolved

Most charges do not end with a finding against the employer. Here is how the EEOC resolved the 37,613 harassment charges it closed in FY2025.

| Resolution Type, FY2025 | Count | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| No reasonable cause | 22,778 | 60.6% |
| Administrative closure | 7,978 | 21.2% |
| Withdrawal with benefits | 3,059 | 8.1% |
| Settlement | 2,878 | 7.7% |
| Reasonable cause found | 920 | 2.4% |

The EEOC calls settlements, withdrawals with benefits, and reasonable-cause findings "merit resolutions." There were 6,857 of them, or 18.2% of harassment resolutions.

A no-cause finding does not mean nothing happened. It means the EEOC did not find enough evidence of a legal violation to continue. The charging party may still be able to file a lawsuit.

Reasonable-cause findings rose from 620 in FY2024 to 920 in FY2025. Of those, 264 ended in a successful conciliation and 656 in an unsuccessful one.

Conciliation is the EEOC's attempt to settle a charge after it finds reasonable cause. When it fails, the agency can decide whether to sue, and the charging party can pursue their own options. So the 656 unsuccessful conciliations are not dead ends. They mark the point where a charge may move toward court.

Settlements and withdrawals with benefits together resolved 5,937 harassment charges in FY2025, by our calculation. That is far more than the number of reasonable-cause findings, which shows that most benefits come from early resolution, not formal findings.

## Sexual Harassment Outcomes

Sexual harassment charges resolve somewhat differently from harassment charges overall. Here is the FY2025 breakdown for the 8,689 sexual harassment charges the EEOC resolved, from Table E2c.

| Resolution Type, FY2025 | Count | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| No reasonable cause | 4,191 | 48.2% |
| Administrative closure | 2,304 | 26.5% |
| Withdrawal with benefits | 973 | 11.2% |
| Settlement | 865 | 10.0% |
| Reasonable cause found | 356 | 4.1% |

Merit resolutions made up 25.3% of sexual harassment resolutions, compared with 18.2% for all harassment charges. In other words, a sexual harassment charge was more likely to end with some benefit to the charging party. The tables show the difference but do not explain it.

## Harassment Within All EEOC Charges

Harassment is not a separate type of discrimination. It is a form of conduct that can be based on sex, race, disability, age, or another protected trait. The EEOC received 88,201 charges of all kinds in FY2025.

By our calculation, harassment allegations appeared in about 42% of all charges (37,397 divided by 88,201). One charge can allege harassment and other issues at once, such as firing or failure to promote. That is why the share is a rough guide, not an exact split.

## Harassment Lawsuits Filed by the EEOC

The EEOC files a small number of lawsuits each year. Its [Office of General Counsel FY2025 annual report](https://www.eeoc.gov/office-general-counsel-fiscal-year-2025-annual-report) lists harassment and sexual harassment as the issue raised most often in the agency's 94 merits suits, appearing in 29 of them.

The same report says the agency achieved a successful outcome in 109, or 96.5%, of its merits suit resolutions, excluding voluntary dismissals without prejudice. It recovered almost $27 million in litigation for about 2,505 people across all suit types.

Lawsuits are rare compared with charges. The EEOC sues in only a small fraction of matters, usually those it considers strong or systemic.

## Why Workplace Harassment Statistics Rise and Fall

A change in the charge count can come from several places, and the tables do not separate them.

1. **Behavior at work.** More or less harassment would change the number of complaints.
2. **Willingness to report.** Public attention, new guidance, or better awareness of rights can lead more people to file.
3. **Access to the agency.** Office staffing, intake systems, and online filing all affect how easily a charge is taken.
4. **Labor market size.** More people working means more chances for both harassment and charges.

The FY2021 dip, for example, came during an unusual period for workplaces. Reading workplace harassment statistics well means holding all four explanations open until other evidence narrows them down.

## Why Charges Undercount Harassment

Charge data capture only people who file. Many never do.

The EEOC's archived data highlight on sexual harassment cites research finding that 90% of individuals who say they have experienced harassment never take formal action. That figure comes from an outside academic study, not EEOC records, and it predates FY2025.

People may stay silent for many reasons, including fear of retaliation. Retaliation is already the most common basis in all EEOC charges, which shows how often workers report it after speaking up.

Charges also miss complaints handled inside a company, through a state agency alone, or in a private lawsuit. The EEOC's table notes say the data do not include charges filed with state or local Fair Employment Practices Agencies.

So rising charge counts can mean two things at once. More harassment may be happening, or more workers may be willing to report it. The table cannot tell which.

## What the Data Cannot Show

EEOC tables cannot tell you:

- **Prevalence:** how common harassment is across all workplaces.
- **Merit of Each Charge:** whether a specific allegation was true.
- **Employer Practices:** which industries or employers have the most problems.
- **Private Outcomes:** settlements reached outside the EEOC.

Surveys of workers can estimate prevalence, but they use different definitions and samples. Keep survey figures and charge counts in separate columns.

## How Employers and Workers Can Use These Numbers

Workplace harassment statistics are most useful as context, not as a forecast for any one workplace.

- **Employers:** The rising charge count is a signal to review reporting channels, training, and how complaints are investigated. National data cannot tell you how your own workplace compares.
- **Workers:** The resolution tables show that most charges end without a finding. That is not proof a claim lacks merit, but it is a reason to keep records and get advice early.
- **Researchers:** Pair EEOC tables with survey data to estimate how many incidents never become charges. Label each source clearly.
- **Journalists:** Name the fiscal year and say "charges," not "cases" or "incidents."

## How to Read Workplace Harassment Statistics

Before you quote workplace harassment statistics, check four things:

1. **Unit:** charges, lawsuits, or survey responses.
2. **Scope:** all harassment or sexual harassment only.
3. **Period:** the EEOC fiscal year, which ends September 30.
4. **Outcome:** a filed charge, or a charge with a finding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How Many Harassment Charges Did the EEOC Receive in 2025?

The EEOC received 37,397 charges alleging harassment in fiscal year 2025, up from 35,774 in FY2024.

### How Many Sexual Harassment Charges Were Filed?

The EEOC received 8,475 Title VII sexual harassment charges in FY2025.

### Is Workplace Harassment Increasing?

EEOC harassment charges have risen each year since FY2021 and reached a series high in FY2025. Charges measure reports, not total incidents.

### How Often Does the EEOC Find Harassment?

In FY2025, the EEOC found reasonable cause in 920 harassment charges, about 2.4% of resolutions.

### Do Most People Report Workplace Harassment?

No. Research cited by the EEOC found that 90% of people who say they experienced harassment never take formal action.
