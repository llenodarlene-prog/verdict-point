# Nursing Home Abuse Statistics: Deficiencies and Enforcement

There is no single national count of nursing home abuse. The best federal evidence comes from inspection and enforcement records. A 2025 HHS Office of Inspector General (OIG) study of the worst-performing nursing homes found that 64% received a serious deficiency within three years of leaving a federal oversight program.

That study covered 645 homes placed in the Special Focus Facility (SFF) program from 2013 through 2022. Together they received 6,296 complaint surveys while in the program. These nursing home abuse statistics describe citations and complaints, which are signs of risk and harm, not confirmed counts of abuse.

This page explains what the federal data show, where the gaps are, and how to read inspection records. We checked every figure against the OIG report and CMS dataset records on November 13, 2026.

## Key Takeaways

- **No Single Abuse Count:** Federal data track complaints, citations, and penalties, not confirmed abuse incidents.
- **Worst Performers Studied:** 645 nursing homes were Special Focus Facilities from 2013 through 2022, less than 5% of all homes.
- **Heavy Complaint Load:** 92% of those homes received complaint surveys, 6,296 in total, while in the program.
- **Relapse Is Common:** 64% of homes that graduated received a serious deficiency within three years.
- **Penalties Dominate:** Civil money penalties made up 57.1% of the 1,670 enforcement remedies imposed on these homes.

## Why There Is No National Abuse Count

Abuse in nursing homes reaches federal records in indirect ways. A resident or family may complain to a state agency. Inspectors may cite a home for failing to prevent abuse or investigate allegations. Police or prosecutors may bring charges.

Each path produces a different record, and none counts every incident. Many cases are never reported. So nursing home abuse statistics should always name their unit: a complaint, a citation, a penalty, or a conviction.

The most useful national data come from inspections of homes certified by Medicare and Medicaid. The OIG report counts 15,136 nursing homes nationwide as of December 2022.

## The Special Focus Facility Program

The OIG's [report on Special Focus Facility nursing homes, 2013 to 2022](https://oig.hhs.gov/reports/all/2025/special-focus-facility-program-nursing-homes-20132022/) describes the program as the nation's flagship effort to improve the poorest-performing homes through closer oversight.

Only a small number of homes take part at any time. Since May 2014, there have been 88 SFF slots and 440 candidates across 49 states. SFFs are inspected every six months, twice as often as other homes. To graduate, a home cannot receive a serious deficiency on two straight standard surveys or on any complaint survey.

| SFF Program Figure, 2013 to 2022 | Count |
|---|---:|
| Homes that were SFFs | 645 |
| Homes that left the program | 563 |
| Graduated | 495 |
| Terminated or closed | 71 |
| Repeated the program more than once | 17 |
| Still in the program for the first time as of 2022 | 82 |

Ownership stood out. The OIG found 88% of SFFs were for-profit at selection, compared with 72% of all skilled nursing facilities nationwide.

## Complaints and Serious Deficiencies

From 2013 through 2022, 591 of the 645 SFFs, or 92%, received a combined 6,296 complaint surveys while in the program. Two-thirds received 10 or fewer. But 15 homes received more than 50, and one received 257.

| Complaint Surveys per SFF | Homes |
|---|---:|
| 1 to 5 | 254 |
| 6 to 10 | 170 |
| 11 to 25 | 135 |
| 26 to 50 | 17 |
| 51 to 100 | 10 |
| 101 to 200 | 4 |
| More than 200 | 1 |

Long stays and heavy complaint loads went together. Of the 15 SFFs that received more than 50 complaint surveys, 13 were in the program for more than two years. The OIG notes that state agencies must conduct onsite surveys of complaints that allege serious injury or harm, so a high complaint count usually means repeated on-site checks.

The single most extreme case shows how far problems can run. One SFF, in the program twice for a total of six years, received 257 complaint surveys, with four Immediate Jeopardy deficiencies. It was terminated in 2022. Cases like this one are rare, but they show why a home's full complaint history matters more than any single inspection.

Complaints often led to citations. In total, 497 SFFs received 1,962 complaint surveys that found deficiencies. Of those surveys, 7% led to an Immediate Jeopardy deficiency, meaning residents were at risk of serious injury, harm, impairment, or death. Another 19% led to a high scope and severity deficiency. Together, that is the 26% the OIG highlights.

## Abuse-Related Problems Before Selection

The OIG looked at the 10 most common deficiencies that 466 homes received in the three years before entering the program. Two relate directly to abuse.

- **Investigating Allegations:** 76% of these homes were cited for failing to properly investigate allegations.
- **Abuse and Neglect Policies:** 66% were cited for deficiencies in abuse and neglect policies.

Infection control and resident well-being led the list, each at 90%. Once homes entered the program, high scope and severity deficiencies fell from 18% to 9%, and Immediate Jeopardy deficiencies from 6% to 2%. Abuse deficiencies stayed at about 1%.

## What Happens After Graduation

Graduation does not mean a home stays fixed. Within the first year after graduating, about one-third of homes received a serious deficiency. By three years, the share climbed to 64%, nearly two-thirds.

Time in the program varied widely. The median stay was 16 months. Of the 429 homes that entered and exited during the study, 96 spent more than two years in the program, about 22%. Among 374 graduates, 87 took two years or longer, even though CMS aims for graduation in under two years.

The OIG's companion report concludes that the SFF program has not yielded lasting improvements for most homes.

## Nursing Home Abuse Statistics by State

The OIG report breaks SFF participation down by state. Larger states get more SFF slots, so they also show more graduations, terminations, and closures. The report says the five largest states represent 30% of all SFFs over the ten years.

| State | SFF Graduates | Terminated or Closed | In Program as of 2022 | Total SFFs, 2013 to 2022 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Texas | 34 | 5 | 6 | 45 |
| Ohio | 29 | 10 | 5 | 44 |
| Illinois | 32 | 2 | 4 | 38 |
| California | 26 | 6 | 6 | 38 |
| Pennsylvania | 27 | 2 | 4 | 33 |

Ohio stands out for terminations and closures, with 10. These are counts of the worst-performing homes in each state, not state abuse rates. A state with more slots will always show higher totals.

Time to graduate also varied by state. Across all states, the overall SFF graduation rate from 2013 through 2022 was 76%. Average time to graduation ranged from 12 months in Kentucky to 42 months in Alabama. In seven states, SFFs took more than 24 months on average to graduate.

## The Longest and Shortest Stays

Individual cases show how uneven the program can be. One New York home spent 54 months, or 4.5 years, as an SFF before graduating. One Illinois home graduated in just under 6 months, the fastest of any SFF.

Among homes that left without graduating, one California home spent 68 months, more than 5.5 years, before it was terminated from Medicare and Medicaid. One Illinois home voluntarily closed after just 3 months.

Graduates and homes that were terminated or closed spent a similar time in the program on average. But a higher share of graduates stayed two years or more: 23% of graduates, compared with 16% of terminated or closed homes.

## Enforcement and Penalties

From 2013 through 2022, CMS imposed and put into effect 1,670 enforcement remedies on SFFs.

| Remedy Type | Share of Remedies |
|---|---:|
| Civil money penalties | 57.1% |
| Directed plans of correction | 16.2% |
| Discretionary denial of payment for new admissions | 15.5% |
| Directed in-service training | 4.7% |
| Mandatory denial of payment for new admissions | 3.4% |
| Termination | 1.5% |

Civil money penalties ranged from $262 to $646,059. Directed plans of correction, which require a home to take specific actions within a set time, were concentrated in a few homes. Seven SFFs received five or more, and two homes still in the program as of 2022 received seven each, the most of any SFF.

Penalties like these are some of the clearest nursing home abuse statistics available, because they record a regulator's formal response to a finding, not just an allegation. CMS terminated 25 SFFs from Medicare or Medicaid, and Ohio had the most terminations, at six.

## CMS Inspection Data You Can Search

CMS publishes inspection records for every certified home. Its [Health Deficiencies dataset](https://data.cms.gov/provider-data/dataset/r5ix-sfxw) lists nursing home health citations from the last three years, one citation per row, with the inspection date, citation tag, scope and severity, and correction date. The record shows it was last modified October 1, 2026.

A related Survey Summary dataset gives one row per home, with dates of the three most recent inspections and citation counts by category.

These are powerful tools, but each row is one citation, not one abuse incident. To count homes or inspections, you must group the rows. A single inspection can produce many citations, and many citations have nothing to do with abuse.

## What These Findings Mean for Families

Nursing home abuse statistics from federal oversight cannot rate one home on their own. They can still help families ask better questions.

- **Check the inspection record.** CMS publishes citations for every certified home. Look for repeated serious deficiencies, not just a single citation.
- **Ask about SFF status.** A home in the program, or recently graduated, has a history of serious problems. The OIG found most graduates slipped back within three years.
- **Look at complaint history.** Many complaint surveys can signal ongoing concerns, though a single complaint is not proof of harm.
- **Ask how allegations are handled.** Before entering the program, 76% of studied homes were cited for problems investigating allegations.

## How to Read Nursing Home Abuse Statistics

Before you quote nursing home abuse statistics, check:

1. **Unit:** complaint, citation, penalty, or confirmed case.
2. **Population:** all homes, or a selected group such as SFFs.
3. **Period:** the years and dataset release used.
4. **Severity:** any deficiency, or only serious ones.

The SFF findings describe the worst-performing homes. They should not be applied to every nursing home.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How Common Is Nursing Home Abuse?

There is no single national count. Federal data track complaints and inspection citations, which signal risk but do not confirm each incident.

### What Is a Special Focus Facility?

It is a nursing home placed in a CMS program for the poorest performers, with inspections every six months instead of yearly.

### How Many Nursing Homes Are in the US?

The OIG counted 15,136 nursing homes nationwide as of December 2022.

### Do Problem Nursing Homes Improve?

Often not for long. The OIG found 64% of SFF graduates received a serious deficiency within three years.

### Where Can I Check a Nursing Home's Inspection Record?

CMS publishes health citations for every certified home in its Health Deficiencies dataset.
